\section{Adversarial Examples and Projected Gradient Methods}
\label{sec:adversarial-examples}
Deep neural networks are known to be vulnerable to small, intentionally crafted perturbations that drastically 
affect model outputs or maximize loss metrics~\cite{szegedyIntriguingPropertiesNeural2014,goodfellowExplainingHarnessingAdversarial2015}. 
This vulnerability arises from the fundamental difference between average-case and worst-case generalization: 
Random sampling may fail to reliably expose inputs associated with large loss values, 
whereas gradient-guided optimization explicitly searches for such directions~\citeSec{12.4}{aggarwalNeuralNetworksDeep2023}. 
By applying gradient ascent directly to the inputs under the constraint of a bounded $\ell_p$-norm 
$\|\delta\|_p \le \varepsilon_\delta$, the approach targets regions where the objective value is large.
In neural control, adversarial search strategies can be adapted to serve as tools for empirical falsification. 
The goal here is not to deceive a model, but rather to identify input configurations that
identify violations of safety or stability requirements. This provides a computationally efficient approach to find 
high violations during training~\cite{yangLyapunovstableNeuralControl2024}.

In supervised learning, the generation of adversarial examples can be formulated as a constrained optimization problem~
\cite{goodfellowExplainingHarnessingAdversarial2015,madryDeepLearningModels2019}. 
Consider a trained model parameterized by $\theta$, a nominal input $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n_0}$, 
and its target value $y \in \mathbb{R}^{n_L}$. 
Under a white-box assumption, the adversary has full knowledge of the network architecture and weights, 
which allows the computation of accurate input gradients $\nabla_x \mathcal{J}$. 
The objective is to find an optimal perturbation vector $\delta \in \mathbb{R}^{n_0}$ 
that maximizes the loss function $\mathcal{J}(x + \delta, y; \theta)$~\cite{madryDeepLearningModels2019}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:adversarial-problem}
    \max_{\delta}
    \quad
    \mathcal{J}(x + \delta, y; \theta)
    \qquad
    \text{s.t.}
    \quad
    \|\delta\|_p \leq \varepsilon_\delta,
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_\delta > 0$ bounds the perturbation size under an $\ell_p$-norm, with typical choices 
being $p \in \{2, \infty\}$~\cite{goodfellowExplainingHarnessingAdversarial2015,madryDeepLearningModels2019}.
In practical applications, the perturbed input may additionally be clipped to
the valid input range.



\subsection{Projected Gradient Descent}
\label{subsec:pgd}
To solve the maximization problem \labelcref{eq:adversarial-problem}, the Fast Gradient Sign Method 
(FGSM)~\cite{goodfellowExplainingHarnessingAdversarial2015} provides a fast, linear one-step approximation by taking a 
single step along the sign of the loss gradient. However, in highly non-convex loss landscapes, 
one-step attacks may yield weaker candidates than iterative attacks~\cite{madryDeepLearningModels2019}.

Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) extends FGSM into an iterative multi-step gradient ascent scheme. 
Following the robust optimization framework proposed by Madry~\cite{madryDeepLearningModels2019}, 
$\mathcal{P}$ denotes the set of allowed perturbations. 
For an $\ell_\infty$-norm constraint, $\mathcal{P}$ forms a hyper-rectangular box centered at the origin:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:perturbation-set}
    \mathcal{P} := \left\{
        \delta \in \mathbb{R}^{n_0}
        \,\mid\,
        \|\delta\|_\infty \leq \varepsilon_\delta
    \right\}.
\end{equation}
Instead of initializing the search at the clean input $x$, the initial candidate $x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(0)}$ is sampled uniformly 
from $x + \mathcal{P}$ to prevent the optimization from getting stuck in suboptimal local maxima~
\cite{madryDeepLearningModels2019}. At each step $j \in \{0, \dots, I-1\}$, the adversarial sample is updated along the gradient direction:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:adversarial-update}
    x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(j+1)}\leftarrow 
        \Pi_{x + \mathcal{P}} \left(
            x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(j)} + \alpha_{\mathrm{PGD}} \operatorname{sign} \left(
                \nabla_{x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(j)}} \mathcal{J} \left(
                    x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(j)}, y; \theta
                \right)
            \right)
        \right),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{\mathrm{PGD}} > 0$ denotes the step size and $I \in \mathbb{N}$ represents the total number of iterations. 
Furthermore, $\Pi_{x + \mathcal{P}}(\cdot)$ projects the updated sample back onto the constraint set~
\cite{madryDeepLearningModels2019}. This projection can be implemented by clipping 
each input component to the interval $[x-\varepsilon_\delta,x+\varepsilon_\delta]$. 
As a result, $x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(j+1)}$ satisfies the perturbation constraint at every iteration.


\subsection{Adaptation to Neural Lyapunov Control}
\label{subsec:pgd-lyapunov-control}

When moving from conventional adversarial attacks to neural Lyapunov control, the adversary's objective fundamentally changes. 
Rather than deceiving a classifier, the goal becomes to identify counterexample states $x_{\mathrm{adv}}$ 
that violate control-theoretic guarantees, such as Lyapunov decrease \labelcref{eq:asymptotic-lyapunov-stability} 
or domain invariance \labelcref{eq:term-positive-invariance}~\cite{yangLyapunovstableNeuralControl2024}.

Instead of maximizing a classification loss~\cite{goodfellowExplainingHarnessingAdversarial2015,madryDeepLearningModels2019}, 
the gradient-based search is utilized to identify states associated with large violations of the Lyapunov conditions.
In the setting further defined in \cref{sec:imitation-learning} and \cref{subsec:lyapunov-network-architecture}, 
the Lyapunov candidate and the controller are parameterized independently as $V_\phi(x)$ and $\pi_\theta(x)$, 
respectively.
Adapting the robust optimization paradigm~\cite{madryDeepLearningModels2019} to deterministic control, 
their joint training can be formulated as a saddle-point problem:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lyapunov-min-max}
    \min_{(\phi,\theta)} 
    \quad 
    \max_{x_{\mathrm{adv}} \in \mathcal{X}} 
    \quad 
    \mathcal{J}(x_{\mathrm{adv}}; \phi,\theta)
\end{equation}
Here, the inner maximization searches for candidate states within the training domain $\mathcal{X}$ 
that yield large violations of the Lyapunov conditions. The objective $\mathcal{J}(x_{\mathrm{adv}};\phi,\theta)$ 
therefore depends on both the Lyapunov-function parameters $\phi$ and the controller parameters $\theta$.
Conversely, the parameters are updated during the outer optimization, to resolve these 
violations~\cite{madryDeepLearningModels2019}.

Since uniform sampling may fail to discover violations in high-dimensional spaces~\citeSec{12.4}{aggarwalNeuralNetworksDeep2023}, 
gradient-guided ascent along $\nabla_{x} \mathcal{J}$ can focus the search on candidate states associated with large 
Lyapunov condition violations. In this context, PGD serves as an adversarial training mechanism, 
iteratively feeding high-violation candidate states back into the optimization to reinforce Lyapunov stability margins~
\cite{madryDeepLearningModels2019,yangLyapunovstableNeuralControl2024}.
